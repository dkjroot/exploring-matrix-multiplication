What this experiment is trying to build

A multiplication recipe specifies which input entries to add or scale, which resulting combinations to multiply, and how to assemble the answers. The fixed numbers in those instructions are called coefficients. Here we build, transform or search for such recipes using exact arithmetic, then check that the resulting algebra agrees with the ordinary matrix-multiplication rule.

Some pages investigate the Coppersmith–Winograd structure, abbreviated CW. It is a mathematical building block used in research on fast multiplication. Its tensor describes a relationship between three groups of coordinates. A tensor is not automatically one ordinary matrix multiplication: it may describe several separate products or a relationship that needs further work before it can be used as a matrix recipe.

A complete coefficient check verifies the proposed algebraic identity, rather than trying only a few input matrices. We also execute small examples to check the program’s outputs and count its work. These are complementary checks: a correct mathematical identity does not prove every detail of the Python interpreter or every possible execution path.

A small glossary: blocks, stages, ranks and other terms
Matrix size and shape
A square matrix of size n has n rows and n columns. A multiplication shape (m,k,n) means an m×k matrix multiplied by a k×n matrix, producing an m×n result. For example, (2,3,4) takes a 2×3 and a 3×4 matrix and produces a 2×4 matrix.
Block
A smaller rectangular piece of a matrix. Splitting a matrix in half along both rows and columns gives four blocks. A multiplication of two blocks is itself a matrix multiplication, so we may apply another recipe to it.
Stage and recursion depth
A stage is the recipe used at one level of the calculation. Depth zero is the original request; depth one is its smaller child problems; depth two is their children. A repeating schedule chooses which recipe each level uses.
Radix and rank
For a square stage, its radix tells us how much each matrix dimension shrinks in a child problem. Radix two means half-sized children. Its rank here counts the child products in the available recipe: Strassen has seven. That is seven block multiplications, not necessarily seven individual-number multiplications. The word rank does not claim that our available recipe is the smallest possible decomposition. Rectangular stages have separate row, inner and column subdivision factors.
Leaf and cutoff
A leaf is a problem we stop splitting and multiply by the direct row-by-column method. The cutoff is our chosen dimension threshold for stopping recursion. A stage that cannot form nonempty blocks also stops, so such a leaf can exceed the threshold. This changes the balance between saved products and extra combination work.
Divisible core and fringe
The core is the largest leading rectangle whose dimensions divide into the current recipe’s equal blocks. The fringe is the remaining rows, columns and shared inner terms. For example, a 5×5 Strassen call recursively multiplies a 4×4 core and explicitly computes the other dot terms. Every deeper level follows the same rule, without adding zeros to enlarge the input. The optional padded baseline instead enlarges the whole request to a compatible schedule size.
Data product and coefficient product
A data product multiplies values that depend on the input matrices. A coefficient product multiplies a value by a fixed number in the recipe, such as 1/2. Both involve work, but only the first kind is used when reporting the recipe’s bilinear product count.
Field step and atomic step
A field step acts on one mathematical number. An atomic step is one of the smaller scalar calls our code exposes. For example, our exact complex multiplication is one field multiplication implemented with four real multiplications and two real sums or differences. Those totals describe the same work at different levels, so adding them together would count it twice.
Exponent and omega (ω)
An algorithm exponent p describes how its arithmetic grows, roughly like n raised to p for large square matrices. An exponent of three means that doubling n gives about eight times the leading work. Omega is the infimum of exponents achievable by matrix algorithms in the stated arithmetic setting; it is not the exponent of every algorithm in this report. Small-range fitted slopes can differ from the theoretical exponent. The expression log₂(7) means “the power p for which 2 raised to p is 7”, approximately 2.807. It captures Strassen’s sevenfold increase in descendant products when the dimensions double.
Recurrence
A rule describing a problem in terms of its smaller children. In T(n)=7 T(n/2)+O(n²), T(n) denotes the work for size n: seven half-size products plus combination work whose growth is bounded by a constant times n². The rule must be justified by the construction; it is not guessed from a graph.
Setup, verification and measurement overhead
Setup prepares or searches for a recipe before applying it to matrix data. Verification checks its answer or algebraic identity. Measurement overhead is the extra work our counters and allocation probes perform. Their costs are reported with their own boundaries so that they are not silently mistaken for arithmetic needed by the multiplication itself.

Pan’s exact aggregation of two products

Each aggregate term multiplies a left sum by a right sum. Both sums contain entries from the two input pairs, and the product contributes to both outputs. Expanding gives two wanted monomials and six unwanted ones. Three families of correction products remove those unwanted terms in pairs by summing along a shared index. For size four we pay for 64 aggregate products and all 48 correction products. Together they give two independent answers.

At a recursive node the 112 block products go to 56 two-slot child calls. Each returned block goes back to the parent term that requested it, including any negative correction coefficients. The largest expanded identity checks 134,217,728 coefficients, including all zeros between outputs. Sparse coefficient equality checks the zero entries without allocating a dense cube.
